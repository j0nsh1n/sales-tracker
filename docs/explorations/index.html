Four directions for the Sales Tracker desktop app

Same ledger, same rules, same job: log who bought what, hand items over as people collect, and check the cash drawer. Each direction is a working prototype you can click through. None of it touches the production app.

Shared fixture and scope 3 products (Honey $12.50/jar, Strawberry jam $6.00/jar, Beeswax candle $8.00 each) and 9 orders from 7 purchasers: 6 outstanding, 3 received, $170.50 still owed, $260.50 of cash expected in the drawer. Every direction offers the same features: log a sale, set received-so-far, edit an order, filter and search, the Money check with a bill count, product setup, and Settings-only deletion behind a typed RESET. Validation messages are shared. Use the Data menu in the black bar on any prototype to see the empty states.

The four hypotheses

A Counter

Hypothesis: the operator is standing at a stall serving whoever is next. The screen should be a queue, and handing over should be one tap.

  • One column, one list: Waiting, then Collected folded away
  • Log a sale is a sheet; Money and Settings are sheets
  • Hand-over is an in-card stepper (−, number, +, All)
  • Large type and targets; oldest waiting first

B Register

Hypothesis: a practised operator logs dozens of orders in a sitting. Keystrokes beat clicks and density beats whitespace, so everything stays on one screen.

  • Three panes: views and totals, the grid, the money rail
  • A command line logs a sale: name, qty, product, paid by
  • Grid cells edit in place; ↑↓ + − a e Enter drive it
  • Status line reports every result and error

C Guide

Hypothesis: the ledger is shared by people who use it rarely (volunteers, family). Nobody should have to learn it: each task is a short walk with one question per screen.

  • Home: status strip and three task cards
  • Log a sale: who, what, how many, paid by, review, done
  • Hand over: pick the person, pick the amount, receipt
  • Chips remove typing where the answer is small

D Tally

Hypothesis: the seller works crate by crate, so the ledger should be laid out by product. Each order is a strip of pips punched as items go out, like a tally card.

  • A board: one column per product, slips underneath
  • Tap a pip to set handed-over; Undo in the toast
  • New slip is added inside its product column
  • The drawer is a strip along the bottom with bill tiles

Critique

A · Counter

Works

  • The waiting queue is the only thing on screen, so the next action is obvious.
  • Steppers commit each tap, so there is no Save to forget.
  • Collected rows are one fold away, satisfying the spec's "rows never disappear" without cluttering the queue.

Might fail

  • With 60 purchasers the queue is a long scroll; search becomes mandatory and the calm turns into hunting.
  • Money and Settings behind buttons means the drawer check is a detour; the operator may skip it.
  • No column headings: comparing owed amounts across people means reading each card.
  • The oldest-first order is opinionated; a seller who serves by name will want alphabetical.

B · Register

Works

  • Logging a sale takes one line of typing and no mouse; the preview line prevents mis-parses before Enter.
  • Every figure the operator might want (owed, left, cash in drawer) is on screen without navigation.
  • Sorting, filtering and per-product views come almost for free from the grid.

Might fail

  • The comma syntax is invisible until read; a first-time user will type "Jim bought 10 honey" and get an error.
  • Single-key shortcuts (+, −, a) fire on the selected row: an easy way to hand over the wrong person's jars if the selection is not where the eye is.
  • 13px text and 30px rows are hostile on a laptop at a market stall in sunlight.
  • On a phone the three panes become tabs and the density advantage disappears; this is a desktop-only design in practice.

C · Guide

Works

  • Error states are nearly impossible: amounts are chosen from what remains, and the review screen catches the rest.
  • The receipt screen ("Jim now has 7 of 10") is a genuine confirmation, not a toast that disappears.
  • Onboarding is the product: the home screen with no products is a single obvious step.

Might fail

  • Five screens per sale is slow. A seller with a line of ten people will resent it by the third.
  • The list is demoted to a link; a spec that calls the order list "the primary surface" would need rewording.
  • Editing an order is a separate flow reached from the list, two hops from home.
  • Chips only cover small integers; fractional units (lb) always fall through to typing.

D · Tally

Works

  • The whole day is visible in one glance: which crate is nearly empty, who is still waiting on each product.
  • Hand-over is direct manipulation with immediate feedback and an Undo; nothing to type.
  • Logging inside a column removes the product picker, the field most often wrong on the current form.

Might fail

  • Pips stop working past ~24 units or for fractions; the stepper fallback breaks the metaphor exactly where quantities are large.
  • Three products fit; eight products means a wide horizontal scroll on every screen size.
  • A tap on the wrong pip changes a figure instantly. Undo lasts five seconds; after that it is an edit.
  • Tk cannot do this layout cheaply: it is the most expensive direction to build and to keep working on Windows and Linux.

Recommendation

A (Counter) as the base, with B's command line as an optional power feature. The spec describes a solo seller handing items to people in front of them; A is built around exactly that moment, is the cheapest to build in Tk, and carries the fewest accessibility risks. What A lacks is fast bulk entry, and that is the one thing B does far better; a single "quick log" line above the queue, parsed the same way, would give A most of B's speed without its density.

C is the right answer if the ledger is shared by people who rarely use it; it is a different product decision, not a styling one. D is the most interesting and the most fun to hand over with, but it is the most expensive, and its metaphor breaks on large or fractional quantities, which the spec explicitly supports.

This is a recommendation, not a decision. Nothing has been chosen or implemented in the production app.

Decisions needed before refining one

  1. Who is at the keyboard?One practised seller (A or B), several occasional helpers (C), or a seller who packs by product (D). This decides the direction more than anything else.
  2. Where is it used?At the stall on a laptop or tablet, or at home afterwards. Outdoor use rules out B's density and argues for A's targets.
  3. Does the order list stay the primary surface?The spec says yes today. C demotes it and D replaces it with a board; either needs a spec change you approve.
  4. Typical quantities and units.If orders are mostly under ~20 whole units, D's pips work. If pounds and fractions are common, D is out.
  5. Is a phone layout required?The product is a Tk desktop app. If a phone-sized window matters, B is the weakest; if not, its density is a strength.
  6. Should Money be a page, a sheet, or always visible?A and C hide it behind an action, B keeps it on screen, D pins it to the bottom. This affects whether the drawer check actually gets done.
  7. Terminology.The prototypes use "purchaser", "hand over", "slip" (D only), "waiting / collected" (A and C). The spec uses "outstanding / received". Pick one set before copy is written.
